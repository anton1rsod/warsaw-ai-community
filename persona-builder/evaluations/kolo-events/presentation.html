Idea under evaluation — kolo.events (Ivan + Marat)

Kolo Events: six personas, one evidence base, seven PIVOT verdicts.

A free events-and-clubs platform for Warsaw's Russian-, Ukrainian- and English-speaking communities — researched with Maksym's validate-idea method (45+ linked sources), then evaluated independently by six community member personas, each in their own voice and with their own scores. None of them saw the overall result, or each other.

Please read this first: these are AI simulations. The six evaluations were written by AI from each member's self-authored persona file. No member has reviewed or endorsed their draft yet. Each was written independently — without seeing the overall result or the other drafts. And all of it is desk research: the next level of certainty only comes from talking to real organizers.

The short version

  1. Everyone reached the same verdict: don't stop — change one thing. The research scored the idea 13 out of 25; the six member evaluations, made independently, scored 12–14. That band means: good idea, wrong money plan.
  2. The one thing to change: start asking organizers for money now. A small paid-tickets test this month, instead of "free first, earn someday".
  3. The need is real. Around 250,000 foreigners live in Warsaw (about 160,000 of them Ukrainians), and finding events genuinely is scattered across 30+ Telegram channels. No product serves this audience today.
  4. But the money plan, as written, is too small. Only about 50 organizers could realistically pay — even if every one of them did, that's roughly €750 a month.
  5. Kolo's superpower is reach. The first 100 users are one cross-post away; the first 20 organizers are a short list of direct messages.
  6. Two homework items for the founders: sit down with 10 organizers — nobody has yet heard one say the admin work actually hurts; and remove the "Locals.md closed down" line from the pitch — our check shows it isn't true.

Standing alone — what each member would tell the founders

Anton Safronov

Built a Warsaw community platform himself
PIVOT · 12/25
Would say GO when a first ticket fee clears and the organizer asks for the next event. Would say STOP if, after 90 days of personal outreach, fewer than five organizers stay active on their own.
  • The hardest part is not the software — it's the first ten events. An empty calendar looks dead, so newcomers leave, so it stays dead. Breaking that loop is hustle, not code: DM ten organizers personally, set up their clubs for them, attend their events, write the recaps.
  • His math: subscriptions from ~50 organizers top out at €100–400 a month. Ticket fees are the real path: five active organizers running two paid events a week ≈ €1,200–2,400 a month — real money, testable in week one.
  • His move: treat the two biggest community channels as partners, not ad space — and make Ukrainian House the first call, not the tenth. One co-branded event buys years of trust.

Дмитрий Блюс

Tech lead · small founding teams
PIVOT · 13/25
Would say GO if 5 of 10 organizers name €10+/month unprompted. Would say STOP if interviews show the real problem is attendance, not admin work.
  • Keep the engineering bill small until the idea earns it. Months of features were built before the hardest question — will anyone pay? — was asked even once.
  • His math: three languages add a permanent ~40–50% tax on every future change; chat moderation costs 2–4 hours a week forever; the planned iPhone/Android apps would eat ~6 months of two people's time before the first paying customer exists.
  • His move: stop the native apps. The web app already works on phones. Spend two weeks interviewing organizers instead — it costs nothing and answers the only question that matters.

Heorhii K.

Builds fast, multi-language platforms
PIVOT · 13/25
Would say GO if 3 of 5 organizers pay AND public pages load in under two seconds on a phone. Would say STOP at zero of five — that means free Telegram has already won.
  • The open feed is Kolo's best free marketing — most teams never realize this. Because events can be browsed without an account, Google can find and rank every event page. Someone searches "Ukrainian language exchange Warsaw" and lands on Kolo for free.
  • His math: one good announcement post ≈ 2,500 visitors; if pages load fast, 15–20% come back — that's 375–500 returning visitors from a single post, with zero ad budget. But slow pages cut that number by a third to a half.
  • His move: make the public pages fast first; lead with one language and let the other two follow a week behind; and charge five organizers €10/month now — failure costs nothing and teaches everything.

Maksym Pavlenko

Payments engineer · wrote the research method
PIVOT · 14/25
Would say GO when three organizers complete paid events without a single support call and one comes back on his own. Would say STOP if everyone who tries goes back to cash-at-the-door.
  • The safe way to take money is simpler than it sounds. Let Stripe hold the money — then Kolo is a middleman, not a bank: no banking license needed, and tax applies only to Kolo's small fee, not the whole ticket.
  • His math: a realistic first month is ~€150–300 in fees — a proof, not a business. The good case (20 organizers, two paid events a month each) reaches ~€1,350 a month. Know that number before planning around it.
  • His move: launch paid tickets before subscriptions (Kolo earns only when the organizer earns); decide the refund and payout rules before launch, not after the first complaint; and log every payment from day one.

Mark Spasonov

Commercial operator · everything in numbers
PIVOT · 12/25
Would say GO when two organizers pay anything within 30 days — even by bank transfer. Would say STOP if six weeks of personal outreach produce fewer than two people interested in paying.
  • The founders are missing a one-page number sheet. Five numbers, checked every Monday: active organizers · events that filled up · people who came back for a second event · organizers still posting after three months · organizers who said yes to paying.
  • His math: the 90-day bar should be binary — 20 active organizers + 3 paying + a quarter of attendees coming back. All three, or change course. Written down, signed, goalposts never moved.
  • His move: split the founders' time 60% selling / 40% building. Selling means messaging organizers and sitting next to them as they work — building only what those conversations say is blocking a payment.

Nazar K.

Growth operator · $1.5M of ad spend behind him
PIVOT · 12/25
Would say GO at three cards on file within 14 days plus 15%+ of new users returning a week later. Would say STOP at zero payments and fewer than 8% returning.
  • A literal 30-day test script: first, tag every link so each signup's source is known; then post to the three biggest channels one by one; then DM 20 organizers with a one-sentence offer; then count exactly three numbers — signups by source, who returned after 7 days, who paid.
  • His math: one cross-post realistically brings ~300 signups; typically 10–25% return a week later — 30 to 75 weekly regulars from a single post. That's not failure, that's data. Five paying organizers ≈ €75/month — a signal, not a business.
  • His move: don't touch the 37,000-follower channel until the smaller tests show people come back. A big audience's attention burns exactly once — spend it when the product is ready to hold it.

Findings

Where all six agree

  • The real incumbent is free Telegram, not Meetup. The wedge vs. paid tools is real; the wedge vs. a free habit is UX only.
  • Pain is documented, but never quoted. 33 fragmented channels, 37k followers on a diaspora events feed — yet zero verbatim organizer-pain quotes survived honest search.
  • Free-first is the flaw — and it's fixable. Every PIVOT lands on monetization timing / price model. Couchsurfing shows monetize-later is also trust-risky later.
  • Distribution is the strongest pillar. First 100 users ≈ one cross-post; first 20 organizers ≈ a DM list.
  • Drop the Locals.md claim. It failed verification (the platform appears alive) — flagged independently by three personas.

Where the panel splits

  • Viability 1 vs 2 — a clean lens split. Commercial seats (Anton, Mark, Nazar) refuse to score above 1 without a single invoice; engineering seats (Dmitriy, Heorhii, Maksym) credit the comparables with a 2.
  • Market 2 vs 3. Maksym credits comparable density; the rest hold at 2 on the bottom-up math.

Timing tailwinds

  • Meetup price shock (2024→) — a live migration list of angry organizers.
  • FB Community Chats killed Oct 2025; organic reach 1–2%.
  • IRL-social wave: Timeleft €18M ARR; Joiner €500k into Poland.

The plan — all six point to the same move: ask organizers to pay, now

STEP 1 · 7 DAYS · €0

Ask 20 organizers to pay

A direct message to 10–20 named Warsaw organizers. One sentence: "Kolo replaces your Telegram signup chaos — €15 a month, cancel anytime." A real payment ask, not a waitlist.

PASS: at least 3 say yes with a card on file
STEP 2 · 30 DAYS

Run real paid events

2–3 organizers who already sell tickets run one event each through Kolo, with a 5–10% fee. Stripe holds the money, so no banking license is needed and tax applies only to Kolo's small fee.

PASS: 3+ tickets sold and paid out + organizer asks to repeat
IN PARALLEL

Honest interviews

Sit down with 10–15 organizers. Ask only about what they actually do and spend today — never "would you use this?". This fills the biggest gap in the research: nobody has yet heard the pain first-hand.

PASS: 7 of 10 name signup admin among their top 3 pains, unprompted
